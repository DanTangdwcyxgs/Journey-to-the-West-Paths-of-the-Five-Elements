# Character Bible

This document defines the five protagonists as both narrative characters and gameplay identities. Character development must reinforce the gameplay role without reducing the character to that role.

## Sun Wukong

**Core conflict:** freedom vs restraint

**Starting fantasy:** unstoppable trickster-warrior.

**Long arc:** Wukong begins by believing that power means nobody can command him. The pilgrimage gradually reframes freedom as the ability to choose responsibility rather than merely reject authority.

**Personality pillars:** proud, curious, loyal to those he accepts, impatient with hypocrisy, capable of genuine tenderness but uncomfortable showing it.

**Combat pillars:** explosive single-target damage, staff techniques, fire/strike interactions, transformation and perception.

**Key relationship:** Tang. Their conflict must feel fundamental: Wukong values action and Tang values purpose.

## Tang Sanzang

**Core conflict:** faith vs reality

**Starting fantasy:** vulnerable leader who survives through preparation and conviction.

**Long arc:** Tang begins with a clear belief that the journey has a moral meaning. Experience forces him to distinguish faith from certainty and compassion from passivity.

**Personality pillars:** disciplined, compassionate, stubborn, intellectually curious, sometimes naive.

**Combat pillars:** healing, purification, protection, barriers, control of enemy behavior, high-risk support decisions.

**Key relationship:** Wukong. Tang must learn when to restrain Wukong and when to trust him.

## Zhu Bajie

**Core conflict:** desire vs responsibility

**Starting fantasy:** charismatic, physically powerful fallen celestial officer.

**Long arc:** Bajie wants comfort and immediate pleasure because responsibility has previously cost him his status. The journey teaches him that responsibility does not have to mean abandoning all desire.

**Personality pillars:** funny, hungry, flirtatious, proud, insecure, surprisingly perceptive about other people's weaknesses.

**Combat pillars:** heavy defense, taunt, damage redirection, disruptive attacks, food/resource bonuses.

**Key relationship:** Wukong. Their rivalry should produce both comedy and genuine mutual respect.

## Sha Wujing

**Core conflict:** guilt vs redemption

**Starting fantasy:** disciplined survivor who wins through endurance.

**Long arc:** Wujing initially believes punishment is simply what he deserves. The pilgrimage gradually teaches him that accepting redemption is not the same as denying guilt.

**Personality pillars:** quiet, practical, patient, observant, dependable.

**Combat pillars:** water control, debuffs, slowing, defensive counters, resource-efficient endurance.

**Key relationship:** Tang. Tang offers Wujing a purpose, while Wujing gives Tang the grounded reliability he sometimes lacks.

## Bai Longma

**Core conflict:** identity vs duty

**Starting fantasy:** mysterious white dragon hidden inside the role of a humble mount.

**Long arc:** Longma begins by allowing others to define him: prince, criminal, mount, servant. His eventual awakening is the decision to define himself.

**Personality pillars:** restrained, proud, intelligent, sensitive to hierarchy, initially distant.

**Combat pillars:** transformation/stance switching, mobility, lightning/water interactions, adaptive weakness coverage.

**Key relationship:** the entire party, but especially Tang. Tang treats Longma as a companion before Longma knows how to see himself that way.

# Cross-Character Writing Rules

1. Nobody is a pure archetype. Each protagonist needs contradictory traits.
2. Recruitment must not erase earlier personality. Joining the party creates new pressure on the character rather than solving the character's problems.
3. Humor should come primarily from personality collisions, not from making a character stupid.
4. Major emotional changes require a gameplay expression whenever practical.
5. Every protagonist needs at least one moment where another character correctly understands a part of them that they cannot articulate themselves.
6. Every protagonist needs one moment where their defining weakness causes a real party problem.
7. The final shared campaign should resolve the five themes together rather than giving five unrelated endings.
